Sakura Saku Dorm Song: My Neighbor from Another World

Dear Bastard Father,

The capital. Cherry blossom season.
I'm in Tokyo — the Tokyo I dreamed about.
I've left my backwater hometown in Kyushu behind, and now I'm climbing a long, long hill — the kind that looks like it runs straight up to heaven — through a residential neighborhood up against the mountains, thirty minutes by train from the neon-lit metropolis (haven't actually seen the neon yet, but give it a few days, I'll get there), and honestly not all that different from back home.
Three years ago, when I started high school, I just assumed I'd go on to university like it was the obvious next step. You declared you'd put me to work at the inn the second I graduated, and we got into an actual fistfight over it. Even that feels kind of nostalgic now.
Then you said that if I wanted experience running a business, I should get through university on my own, with zero help from you — and I declared I'd take that challenge head-on. I regret that now.
I never imagined my wallet would vanish into thin air the moment I arrived at Tokyo Station.
Did you know? Tokyo Station is huge. Rows and rows of shops I'd never seen before, and there I was, starving, agonizing over which fancy place to have my commemorative first meal at. Right up until I realized there was nothing in my pants pocket.
Did you know? Tokyo Station has a police box, too. Inside the station. So the first person I talked to in Tokyo was a cop at the police box. To beg him for train fare.
The officer was kind enough to tell me to call home. I didn't have a cell phone, so he even looked up the number in a flimsy little phone book for me. For the first time in my life, I was grateful the family business was a hot spring inn. Right up until you, in your boundless cruelty, told me to deal with it myself.
Bastard father.
I regret how that call went. I should have swallowed my pride and groveled to you, instead of slamming the phone down (not that I literally threw the receiver — I just hung up with that much force).

Forget it. Writing letters in my head isn't going to reach the old man anyway, and even if I sent one, it wouldn't mean a thing.
The officer, bless him, "gave" me the train fare. Guess he felt sorry for me after my own flesh and blood hit me with that ice-cold response.
You hear that, old man? A total stranger pitied me. Thanks to him, I transferred from the Chuo Line to the Sobu Line and made it all the way here to Karisaka.
But I still haven't reached my destination.
How many more minutes of climbing — until this hill finally ends?
The suitcase I'm dragging feels like it's full of bricks. There's no way a hill like this exists in Tokyo. Maybe coming to Tokyo was all a dream, and I'm actually still back in Oita.
Damn it, I'm thirsty and I'm starving. I should have ditched the pride and borrowed food money from that cop, too.
The people who look like locals are going up and down this hill like it's nothing. Makes you think Tokyo people might have way stronger legs and backs than us country folk.
But their stares were getting to me.
Sure, I get it — hauling luggage this huge up a hill looks weird. There's a bus, after all. It's already passed me twice.
But that wasn't it. It wasn't mockery — "country boy underestimated Tokyo, huh" — and it wasn't pity for some poor broke kid, either.
How do I put it... it was like they were looking at something really rare — not at some kid who'd just moved to Tokyo, but at some kind of exotic animal — with this unease about having to be involved with it from here on. Does that make sense?

Anyway, whatever. Forget that for now.
What kept my half-dead body moving was sheer spite — no way was I letting anyone see me collapse in a heap on my first day of freedom — and the hope that once I got over this hill, I'd reach my new home, the castle I'd be living in from here on out.
Sakura Saku Dorm. What a lucky name (in Japan, passing your university entrance exam is called "sakura saku" — the cherry blossoms bloom). It's like the place itself is celebrating my glorious future.
Honestly, the photos posted online, and the rent — twenty thousand yen a month for a one-room apartment in Tokyo — were a couple of small red flags. Apparently the building is insanely old.
But hey, it's Tokyo, right?
It's not like it'd be some abandoned ruin or anything. It was nearly full when I applied, too. Probably just an old house with a lot of years on it, like a traditional folk house or something. I'm not asking for much. As long as it has a roof, I'm good.
Well — AC would be nice. And a TV. And an internet connection, at least—

I can't. My legs finally stopped on me.
With my arms creaking in pain, I hauled the suitcase up and sat down on it. This was no time to care about people staring. If I didn't rest, I was seriously going to pass out.
"...Damn... I'm so thirsty..."
I wasn't talking to anyone. Strictly muttering to myself — while thinking how nice it would be if some kind soul overheard and brought me water.
"Hohoho."
A voice. Water had arrived!
"Thank you so much!"
I turned around, gratitude ready, and there stood a refined, intellectual-looking old man.
In his hands... no water.
No water, huh.
On second look, he wasn't all that refined, and he didn't look all that intellectual either. Just some old guy.
"Where're you headed with all that luggage, son? Just moved to Tokyo?"
"Uh, yeah, kind of..." Damn, my throat hurts. "I'm starting at Musashi University."
"Ahh, MushaDai, is it. Well, congratulations."
The old guy laughed — hohoho. So people who actually laugh like that really do exist.
"Thank you." Him congratulating me honestly made me happy. My throat still hurt. "Anyway, I'm moving into this place called Sakura Saku Dorm."
The old man froze.
I waited a bit. He didn't move. Did he just up and die? Did this steep hill finally push his body past its limit?
"...Son. You're moving into Sakura Saku Dorm?"
Good. He was alive.
"Yeah, pretty much. Is it famous or something?"
"No, uh... listen. Could you forget you ever met me here?"
"Huh?"
"We won't be crossing paths again anyway. Here — take this as a parting gift. Eat it."
"Wait, hold on. Sakura Saku Dorm is just a normal dorm, right?"
"Fugafuga. Ah — sorry, sorry. My hearing's gone awful lately."
"'Fugafuga'!? What the hell was that!?"

The old man shoved something he'd pulled from inside his coat into my hands and hurried off. Uphill, and at a shocking speed, too. Yeah, that guy's not dying anytime soon... Anyway, what did he give me?
...A salted daifuku.
I ate it. Devoured it.
And its saltiness and sweetness made me groan out loud.
That wasn't... harassment, right?
But thanks to those calories, somehow — somehow — I finally managed to climb all the way to the top of the hill.
The sight waiting for me there knocked me straight to the edge of despair.
I thought the photos online had prepared me. But seeing it in person, it was dilapidated beyond anything I'd imagined — off in a direction I hadn't even thought to imagine.
This is a cultural heritage site at this point. The government is surely protecting it. If someone told me they make money off tour admission fees, I wouldn't be surprised at all.
How it had managed to exist this long without collapsing was a mystery. It even looked like it was leaning a little.

"...Well, it's fine. People live here, so it can't be that dangerous... probably."

Telling myself that, I staggered toward the building's entrance. My thirst had hit its limit and my vision was blurring. And the moment I took my final step, my body hit its limit for real. To put it plainly: I collapsed onto the very old wooden floor.

I heard footsteps pounding toward me.

"Whoa, whoa — passing out right here!?"

Through my fading consciousness, I heard a voice — flustered, but somehow amused.

"Oh my... poor thing."

A woman's voice, gentle and composed.

"Hey, are you alright? Can you hear me?"

A young woman's voice reached me, clear as day.

I scraped together the last of my strength and croaked out the words.

"...Water..."

"Oi! Water! Yuki-chan — get me the coldest one we've got!"
"On it!"

More hurried footsteps. Right away, a cup was pressed to my lips. I gulped it down like my life depended on it. Cold. Ridiculously cold. But it was water! My life was saved!

I caught my breath and finally looked around me.

Three people were sitting there, surrounding me.
A guy with a vacant look on his face.
A graceful, elegant woman.
And a girl with translucent, impossibly white skin.

That was my first meeting with them, more or less.
In short: they'd seen me at my most pathetic.
